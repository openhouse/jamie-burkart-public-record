# WOW List relational curation and participation practice

## Central finding

WOW List was both a working event-discovery product and a practice of
**relational curation**. People made local activity findable, explained why it
mattered, invited others to contribute, and distributed the work of keeping
calendars useful. Software supplied editable events, followable lists and
people, locality, saved and going signals, embeds, and personalized delivery.
Facilitators, guest writers, editors, organizers, maintainers, and participants
supplied welcome, judgment, context, reciprocity, and repair.

Jamie Burkart and Richard Caceres are publicly credited as co-builders. Guest
writers, editors, list maintainers, venues, organizers, and participants also
made the system socially operative. Leadership here is best described as
designing and tending the conditions for distributed participation, not as
sole authorship of other people's activity or community outcomes.

## Evidence frame

This page combines public product records, public support exchanges, an
independent organizer tutorial, an adopter page, and a governed private-source
summary. Those sources establish a recurring operating model. They do not
establish every private interaction, a complete adoption census, equal access,
current product availability, or community impact.

The phrase “relational curation” is a present-day archival synthesis, not a
historical WOW List slogan; intended practice is not uniform experience.

## Social practice

### Open contribution and local autonomy

WOW List was organized around editable events and followable keyword lists.
The public record describes people adding events, placing one event on multiple
lists, following local calendars, and receiving a personalized weekly email.
The design allowed spaces, bands, projects, scenes, and communities to make
their own activity legible while participating in a wider network.

Open contribution created a maintenance obligation. Someone still had to
welcome newcomers, explain the model, correct data, support organizers, tend
categories, and maintain the technical system. “Community-generated” is
therefore incomplete unless it also names stewardship and administrative
power.

### Service before an ask

The governed practice record describes a relational outreach loop: notice an
organizer's work, add or improve an event, tell the organizer, explain why it
was included, and offer help before asking that person to join or maintain a
list. Adoption was tied to demonstrated usefulness, not only promotion.

This can create reciprocity and trust. It is also time-intensive and can place
uneven, often invisible labor on facilitators and maintainers.

### Human onboarding

Signup practices joined direct access with explanation and personal help
finding or creating a first list. The important unit was not merely a new
account; it was a first meaningful relation to a person, place, project, or
interest.

[Shelby Turner's public tutorial](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nQg47LtixPI)
provides independent evidence of an organizer-facing learning layer. It does
not establish platform-wide adoption, uniform support, or current operation.

### Editorial correspondence

Weekly email introductions placed a personal or collective voice before event
recommendations. Guest writers temporarily hosted the message; editors
previewed and revised the digest; and recurring editorial work made event data
feel more like correspondence than an automated alert.

A historical guest label or public appearance does not establish authorship,
rights, or permission to republish a person's words; visibility is not consent.

### Explainable discovery

WOW List connected events to readers through followed lists and people,
locality, mentions, saved events, plans to go, membership, and delivery
settings. Public support exchanges describe profiles, followed local lists,
adding events, and a weekly email. This reflects a social theory: relevance is
relational and situated rather than reducible to one global popularity score.

The system could help explain why an event reached a reader. It could not make
the underlying categories, moderation, or visibility neutral.

### Return to real-world gathering

The project's public provenance connects WOW List to calendars made at Sunday
Dinner. Its social practices repeatedly aimed beyond information retrieval:
discovery was meant to help people meet, support local activity, and sustain
connections across scenes. The [first public WOW List provenance
post](https://x.com/wowlist/status/433671630837919744) credits Richard and
Jamie and says the project grew from those calendars.

## Practice and product-affordance map

| Social practice | Product affordance or artifact | Participation function | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Personal outreach | Editable events and lists | Creates a useful reason to contact an organizer | Guidance does not prove every contact occurred |
| Local hub facilitation | Followable lists, multi-list events, embeds | Connects local calendars without requiring one editor to know everything | Hubs can still concentrate attention and maintenance power |
| Human onboarding | Join flow, profiles, first follows, help messages | Turns registration into an oriented first connection | No uniform-service or accessibility claim |
| Guest hosting and editing | Introduction before recommendations | Gives the digest a situated voice | Labels do not establish authorship or republication permission |
| Explainable relevance | Follows, locality, mentions, saved and going states | Connects an event to a reader through legible relations | Stored relations do not prove reception or impact |
| Public support | Replies and organizer tutorials | Makes product knowledge shareable | Public exchanges are not a complete support census |
| Distributed maintenance | Event editing, list tending, data and software repair | Lets many people contribute to useful continuity | Builders and administrators retain consequential authority |

## How participation moved

The surviving record supports a reusable sequence, with exits and loops rather
than a mandatory funnel:

1. **Encounter:** a person meets a facilitator, event, list, weekly email,
   tutorial, or public support exchange.
2. **Orientation:** a host, introduction, or explanation makes the invitation
   legible.
3. **Contribution:** the person adds or edits an event, follows or maintains a
   list, writes an introduction, or joins a meeting.
4. **Shared representation:** the contribution becomes a structured event,
   curated relation, editorial voice, or note.
5. **Circulation:** feeds, embeds, notifications, public posts, and email carry
   it across existing networks.
6. **Convening:** discovery creates a possibility of attendance, discussion,
   care, or collective action.
7. **Continuation:** participants maintain the record, invite another person,
   or carry a relationship into a new gathering or project.

## Power, limits, and unanswered questions

- Intended practice is not uniform experience. Guides, public support, and
  product affordances do not prove how each participant encountered them.
- Visibility is not consent. A public event, post, tutorial, guest label, or
  archived page does not authorize unrelated reuse of a person or relationship.
- Open contribution did not erase the builders' and administrators' power over
  software, categories, moderation, editorial workflow, and continued operation.
- The record does not establish equal access across disability, language,
  technology, time, geography, class, race, or scene position. No historical
  WCAG conformance claim is made.
- Historical database counts establish product activity, not current users,
  retention, attendance, endorsement, or causal impact.
- The private archive remains incomplete, and collaborator testimony is still
  needed to allocate editorial, technical, outreach, and facilitation labor
  more precisely.
